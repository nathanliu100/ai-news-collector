## 🔭 One Big Question This Week

**If "bigger models" are no longer the moat, what is?**

A few stories this week looked like separate noise, but together they point at the same question.

Zhipu released GLM-5.3. The interesting part isn't its score — it's the method: the base model is *identical* to the previous GLM-5.2, same parameters, same architecture, and the jump came almost entirely from about a month of post-training (RL). Its AA intelligence index went from 53 to 60, straight into the global top tier. Founder Tang Jie posted a blunt line on X: "The trillion-parameter race was a detour for the entire field."

That line carries more weight than GLM-5.3 itself. For three years the industry's default story was: to get a smarter model, stack more parameters and burn more compute. Scaling Law was treated like a law of physics. Now someone ran a clean controlled experiment and showed you the same brain, with a different training recipe, scores 7 points higher — at a fraction of the cost.

So the question follows: if a model's "size" is no longer the moat, where did this week's money go?

The answer is hiding in three other headlines. SpaceX spent $60 billion on Cursor — a code editor. Stripe spent $7-8 billion on OpenRouter — a middleware that routes developers to 400+ models, valued at just $1.3 billion five months ago. Broadcom is assembling up to $100 billion in debt to buy chips for companies like Anthropic.

See the pattern? Nobody is paying a premium for "a bigger model" anymore. The money is all flowing toward "the layer closest to people" — the editor closest to developers, the router closest to money, the chip distributor closest to compute.

That's the most important turn of the week: AI's center of value is shifting from "who's smarter" to "who's closer."

This logic isn't new. Economics has the "smiling curve" — manufacturing is the thinnest margin, while profit pools at the R&D end and the market end. AI is replaying the same script: training a model has gone from "moat" to "infrastructure" — essential, but accessible to everyone, and thin on margin. The real value sits at the two ends.

For those of us making content, this translates to plain language: **stop staring at which video model gained another 2% in quality — watch who can get your video from "idea" to "delivered" the fastest.**

Look at what the Chinese players did this week, all following this logic. Kling's Q2 revenue hit ¥850M, up 200% — not because its image quality suddenly crushed everyone, but because it wired up MCP/CLI so agents can batch-schedule video generation. It turned itself from a "generator" into a "programmable content factory." ByteDance's Seedance Studio went further, building the whole chain — creative planning → storyboard → generation → final cut — into one workspace. MiniMax's Design tool said it outright: creation should shift from "pixel editing" to the "semantic layer" — you express intent, it understands the whole project, and keeps editing until delivery.

The common thread: **none of them are competing on "models." They're competing on "scenes" and "workflows."** Because the capability gap between models will be wiped out within half a year by post-training, open-sourcing, and price cuts. But whoever first understands how a promo-material team actually works — they've embedded themselves into a spot nobody can replace.

So here's a question to leave you with: if one day every video model hits the same quality ceiling — and that day is probably coming faster than you think — which part of your job is the model unable to replace? Is it "writing great prompts," or "knowing, better than anyone else, who this video is for and why it should be cut this way"?

My answer leans toward the latter. Models get cheaper and more "anyone-can-use" every week, and the gap between "people who can use the tool" and "people who know what to use it for" — that crack is the new moat.
