## 🔭 One Big Question This Week

**When AI makes "making" nearly free, what gets more expensive instead?**

Two seemingly contradictory things happened this week.

On one side, Game Science's Yang Qi announced that the studio's next title, *Black Myth: Zhong Kui*, would **deliberately avoid AIGC and be "handcrafted"** through the design and asset stages. He invoked Nolan sticking to film: "There's no last train you're obligated to catch when it comes to tech trends," and said bluntly that AIGC assets are commonly rough in detail, homogenized in style, and lacking in human emotion. On the other side, miHoYo's AI companion product *BSide: Olivia Lin* shut down less than a month after launch — compute couldn't break even, retention collapsed, and emotional value wouldn't fit into a spreadsheet. Two anime-style "AI girlfriends" went down in a row.

One is rejecting AI, the other was burned by it. But set them side by side and you'll notice they're telling the same story.

Our intuition says: the stronger AI gets, the faster we should jump on it. Reality may run the other way. **When tools make "making" nearly free, making itself stops being valuable — what becomes valuable is everything behind it that can't be replicated.**

ByteDance's *Bu Wen Fan Chen* already proved that 20 people plus Seedance can "handcraft" a game with 100% AI-generated visuals in a year. Once "being able to make a game" is no longer scarce, the moat shifts from "can you make it" to "can you make the kind of thing only you can make." Yang Qi refusing AI isn't backward — it's clear-eyed. Nolan's film, Yang Qi's handcrafting: same logic. **When tools become homogenized, refusing the tool becomes the differentiator.** AI can copy the form but not the spirit, and those unreplicable details are exactly the seam between a masterpiece and a knockoff.

miHoYo's lesson is the flip side of the same coin. AI companions never lacked novelty — they lacked "a reason users can't leave." Emotional value is hard to sell because it can't be quantified into a repeatable metric. You can make the AI chattier, prettier, more talented, but the anchor of "companionship" was never in capability — it's in the "human." That's also why the "granddaughter pastes grandma into Journey to the West" video moved everyone: no uncanny valley, no flex, just a familiar face and a nostalgia filter.

This is the "taste leverage" Wan Weigang keeps talking about. When output is no longer the problem, **judgment — what's worth doing, what's worth handcrafting, what's worth handing to AI — becomes the only scarce means of production.** The most valuable person in the AI era isn't the one who can generate, but the one who knows what *not* to generate, and what to do themselves.

For a game-multimedia-assets team like ours, this is both a warning and an opportunity. The warning: if everyone uses the same Seedance and the same Kling to churn out "AI-flavored" assets, your work drowns in homogeneity and you end up competing on who writes prompts faster. The opportunity: the assets that actually land are always the final 10% of human flavor AI can't produce — the detail that makes people instantly recognize "this is ours."

So here's the open question: maybe the sexiest job title of the future won't be "AI prompt engineer," but "person with taste." The real question is — can AI ever learn taste?
